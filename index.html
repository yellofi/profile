How I approach AI R&D

Build better AI. Write better problems.

I treat a dataset as a problem book: real-world cases, well-defined answers, and a way to test what the AI can actually solve. By examining baseline failures, I improve both the problem book and the solver—clarifying whether the next bottleneck lies in the data, the learning task, or the technology.

01

Write the right problems

Cover real-world cases, agree on the answers, and define how success will be judged.

02

Learn from the wrong answers

Read baseline failures to distinguish gaps in the problem book from limits of the solver.

03

Improve the book and the solver

Build better labeling tools and learning targets; refine or redesign the AI, then test the full outcome.

From data-centric development to a biomarker-agnostic architecture.

When model-centric experiments did not yield meaningful gains in early tumor segmentation, I investigated failure cases and labeling criteria to improve performance through data-centric development. I then designed an integrated cellular-compartment architecture for biomarker-agnostic analysis and led system-level improvements.

Data-centric development → Integrated architecture → System-level optimization

Explore the iterations and decisions

01 · Establish performance through data-centric development

In early tumor-segmentation development, I explored segmentation-model lightweighting, multi-input model architectures, and staining-augmentation-based semi-supervised learning. These model-centric approaches did not produce meaningful performance gains at that stage. I shifted toward data-centric methods: analyzing failure cases, enriching training data for underperforming cases, and reaching agreement on ambiguous annotation criteria. This deeper investigation clarified what the AI needed to recognize and drove practical performance improvements.

02 · Develop tools and a quality-controlled dataset

I contributed substantial cell annotation, applied active-learning-inspired data sampling, and independently developed SAM-assisted tissue and cell labeling tools for colleagues to use in dataset construction. Annotation consensus and ongoing quality review formed the basis of a more reliable cell dataset. During cell-dataset development, I distinguished noisy and hard samples and used curriculum learning to stabilize performance. Once the dataset matured, SOTA-based architecture updates and new instance-separation algorithms produced substantial gains—showing that the effective intervention changed with the development stage.

03 · Design an integrated architecture for biomarker-agnostic analysis

To support fully quantitative membrane analysis, I needed to measure staining intensity and completeness rather than assign fixed cell-level classes. After a feasibility test exposed the impracticality of directly annotating stained membranes, I transformed cell and nucleus instance annotations into subcellular pseudo labels and personally designed Universal Cellular Compartment Segmentation: an integrated architecture that jointly identifies nucleus, cytoplasm, and membrane for biomarker-agnostic cell analysis. This separates structural recognition and continuous staining measurement from biomarker-specific interpretation. In a related task, I also used edge-filter pseudo labels to prototype and transition tissue-foreground identification to deep learning.

04 · Own the system-level tradeoffs

I evaluated the end-to-end pipeline separately from its component models. For PD-L1, normalized membrane staining measurements were thresholded into positive and negative tumor cells to calculate TPS. Using external hospital data, I analyzed discrepancies with pathologists and independently set improvement priorities across data, models, and processing algorithms for over a year, ultimately achieving the target level of agreement. Exploratory prototyping had helped clarify the measurement approach; self-initiated seminars on computational pathology and IHC further developed my understanding of the system and its broader technical direction.

From a measurement bottleneck to shared R&D capability.

I redesigned the measurement architecture and learning objective around angle agreement. I carried forward a curriculum-learning strategy from DeepBio, connecting confidence-guided sample selection, a centrally hosted labeling tool, and second-stage training to optimize final performance. Alongside this work, I proposed and led shared VFM architecture research with standardized benchmarks and evaluation protocols.

Task redesign → Confidence-guided annotation → Curriculum learning → Shared capability

Explore the iterations and decisions

01 · Start from the final measurement

The final objective was agreement in spinopelvic angle measurement. The existing multi-stage pipeline combined spine–pelvis segmentation, vertebra-wise landmark detection, and angle calculation. When improvements within that structure proved insufficient, I reconsidered both the prediction architecture and its alignment with the final measurement error.

02 · Align the learning problem

I designed a DINOv3-based single-model architecture that predicts directions aligned with angle error, followed by angle calculation. The shift connected learning more directly to the desired measurement rather than relying on a chain of intermediate tasks.

03 · Transfer the data-and-learning strategy

Building on curriculum-learning experience at DeepBio, I used landmark (anchor) confidence to select noisy and hard samples. I developed a keypoint labeling tool and hosted it on a central server, enabling rapid annotation of approximately 1,000 cases. I then used a curriculum-learning approach for a second training phase to optimize final measurement performance. This connected failure analysis, targeted annotation, and staged training in one improvement cycle.

04 · Make the experiments comparable

I proposed and led R&D of a shared VFM-based architecture for musculoskeletal X-ray tasks. I designed the research plan, benchmark datasets, VFM comparison groups, and evaluation protocols, and conducted SSL experiments to develop candidate models. I planned the validation pipeline and delegated its implementation to a teammate while retaining responsibility for the overall research direction.

05 · Carry insight into the next task

Foundation-model, VLM, and MLLM surveys and PoCs at DeepBio had given me a basis for evaluating methods, benchmarks, and training costs. At Connective, I connected that experience to dataset standardization, more efficient development across tasks, and potential multimodal expansion. With AI-assisted experimentation, I tested more alternatives and organized the technical contributions into MICCAI 2026 papers, including a first-author Early Accept.

What carries forward: domain understanding · annotation tools · data standards · evaluation frameworks · shared architectures
